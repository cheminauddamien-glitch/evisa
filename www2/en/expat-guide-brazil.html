Complete Expat Guide to Brazil 2026: Visa, Residency, Tax & Healthcare

Brazil pairs a low cost of living with beach cities, a huge domestic market and, since 2022, a genuine digital nomad visa. It suits remote workers, retirees on a moderate pension and small investors far more than anyone chasing a zero-tax base, since Brazil taxes worldwide income once you become a resident.

Updated: 3 September 2026 : Editorial Team, eVisa-Card.com

2026 snapshot

The digital nomad visa (VITEM XI, Resolução Normativa CNIg 45/2021) requires USD 1,500/month income or a USD 18,000 bank balance, granted for 1 year, renewable once. Registration with the Polícia Federal is due within 90 days of arrival. The retirement visa (VITEM XIV/VIPER) needs a pension of at least USD 2,000/month. Brazil has no capital controls stopping property purchase by foreigners.

Source: Itamaraty

Who Brazil Is Best For

Brazil suits remote workers on modest USD or EUR income, retirees with a pension above USD 2,000/month, and anyone drawn to Sao Paulo's job market, Rio's lifestyle or Florianopolis's mix of beaches and tech scene. It is a poor fit for anyone seeking a zero-tax base: once you cross 183 days of presence, worldwide income becomes taxable.

Note: Portuguese is essential beyond the largest cities; English is limited outside business districts and tourist zones.

Step by Step

  1. Apply for the visa at a Brazilian consulate abroad, or check eligibility for the online e-visa/visa portal for your nationality — see current Brazil visa requirements, the Brazil e-visa process and visa fees.
  2. Enter Brazil and, within 90 days, book an appointment with the Polícia Federal (gov.br/pf) to register.
  3. Receive the Carteira de Registro Nacional Migratório (CRNM), the physical foreigner ID valid nationwide.
  4. Get a CPF tax number (see Banking section below) — needed for almost everything.
  5. Renew before expiry; check extension rules and typical processing times.
Pro Tip: Missing the 90-day Polícia Federal registration triggers fines and can block visa renewal — book the appointment the week you land, slots fill up in Sao Paulo and Rio.

Digital Nomads

The VITEM XI digital nomad visa, created under CNIg Normative Resolution 45/2021, lets remote employees and freelancers working for foreign clients live in Brazil for one year, renewable once for a total of two. Proof of income (USD 1,500/month) or savings (USD 18,000) plus health insurance valid in Brazil and a clean criminal record are required. Florianopolis and Sao Paulo have the strongest co-working and community scenes; Rio combines lifestyle with a growing tech hub in Porto Maravilha.

Note: The visa forbids working for a Brazilian employer; income must come from abroad.

Working Locally

Taking a job with a Brazilian company requires the employer to sponsor a VITEM V work visa and permit through the Ministry of Labour and Employment, typically for roles that justify hiring a foreigner over a local candidate. Freelancing for Brazilian clients while on a tourist or digital-nomad visa is not permitted. Registering a local business (MEI or LTDA) is possible for residents holding a CRNM and CPF.

Retirement

The VITEM XIV route (often called VIPER once residence is granted) is open to pensioners of any age who can prove a pension of at least USD 2,000/month, transferable to a Brazilian bank account, plus USD 1,000 for each dependant included. It leads directly to a renewable permanent-type residence permit, reviewed after the first years, and is one of the more accessible retirement routes in Latin America.

Family & Dependants

Spouses, registered partners, minor children and dependent parents can be added to most visa applications, including the digital nomad and retirement routes, generally without a separate income threshold beyond a modest top-up. Each dependant registers separately with the Polícia Federal and receives an individual CRNM.

Healthcare

The Sistema Unico de Saude (SUS), run by the Ministry of Health, is free and universal, and by law covers every foreigner present in Brazil — tourists, residents and undocumented migrants alike — at public clinics (UBS) and hospitals, no visa status or payment required. Quality is strong for emergencies and primary care but waiting lists for specialists and elective procedures can be long, which is why most expats who can afford it combine SUS with private insurance.

Health Insurance

Private plans (Amil, Bradesco Saude, SulAmerica, Hapvida) shorten waits and add private-hospital access; a mid-tier plan for a single adult in Sao Paulo runs roughly BRL 400-900/month depending on age and coverage. Digital nomad visa applicants must show insurance valid in Brazil for the full stay before travelling; check current requirements on the consular portal.

Banking & the CPF

The CPF (Cadastro de Pessoa Fisica), issued by the Receita Federal, is Brazil's individual taxpayer number and is required to open a bank account, sign a lease, get a phone plan or receive a salary. Residents apply in person at a Banco do Brasil, Caixa Economica Federal or Correios branch for a small fee; non-residents can apply at a Brazilian consulate or online via the Receita Federal e-CAC portal. With a CPF and CRNM in hand, opening an account at Banco do Brasil, Itau, Nubank or Inter is straightforward; digital banks like Nubank are popular with expats for English-friendly apps and low fees.

Tax Residency

You become a Brazilian tax resident after 183 days, consecutive or not, within any rolling 12-month period; holders of a permanent, work, retirement, investor or digital nomad residence visa are treated as tax resident from the date of arrival, per the Receita Federal. Residents are taxed on worldwide income under the progressive IRPF table: since Law 15,270/2025, income up to BRL 5,000/month is tax-free, tapering to the top marginal rate of 27.5% on higher brackets. Brazil has double-taxation treaties with several countries but not with the US; check your home-country position before relying on any credit.

Housing: Renting & Buying

Foreigners, resident or not, may buy urban property freely with a CPF, subject to standard notary (cartorio) registration and transfer tax (ITBI, roughly 2-3%). Rural land purchases are capped and require prior authorization (Lei 5.709/1971, confirmed applicable by the Supreme Federal Court), and land inside the 150km border strip needs clearance from the National Defence Council. Renting typically requires a Brazilian guarantor (fiador), a security deposit (caucao) or a rental-guarantee insurance policy in lieu of a guarantor.

Cost of Living

A one-bedroom apartment in central Sao Paulo or Rio runs roughly BRL 2,500-4,500/month (indicative, editorial estimate, September 2026); Florianopolis and smaller cities are 20-30% cheaper. A single person's monthly budget outside rent, including groceries, transport and eating out, is typically BRL 2,000-3,500. For official inflation and household-spending data, consult IBGE.

Transport & Driving Licence

Sao Paulo and Rio have metro networks; most other cities rely on buses and ride-hailing (Uber, 99). A foreign driving licence plus an International Driving Permit is valid for up to 180 days; after that, or once you hold a CRNM, you must exchange it for a Brazilian CNH via the Detran of your state.

Schools & Education

Public schools are free but taught in Portuguese; most expat families in Sao Paulo and Rio choose international or bilingual schools (British, American, Swiss curricula), with annual fees typically BRL 30,000-70,000. Florianopolis has fewer international options, making it more suited to remote workers without school-age children.

Phone & Internet

A CPF is required to buy a local SIM (Vivo, Claro, TIM); prepaid plans with several GB of data cost around BRL 40-70/month. Home fibre internet in major cities is fast and affordable, typically BRL 80-150/month, making Sao Paulo, Rio and Florianopolis solid bases for remote work.

Safety & Emergency Numbers

Petty crime and occasional violent crime are real concerns in parts of Sao Paulo and Rio; standard precautions (avoiding certain favelas at night, discreet phone/jewellery use) go a long way. Florianopolis is consistently rated among Brazil's safest cities. Emergency numbers: 190 (police), 192 (ambulance/SAMU), 193 (fire brigade).

Moving Checklist

  1. Confirm the right visa route and gather proof of income/pension/investment.
  2. Get health insurance valid in Brazil before departure.
  3. Book the Polícia Federal appointment as soon as you land (90-day deadline).
  4. Apply for a CPF, then open a bank account.
  5. Arrange housing; expect a fiador or guarantee insurance if renting.
  6. Register with SUS locally and consider a private health plan.

FAQ

What is the minimum income for Brazil's digital nomad visa?

USD 1,500/month in income, or a bank balance of at least USD 18,000, under CNIg Resolution 45/2021. The visa is granted for one year and can be renewed once.

How long do I have to register with the Polícia Federal after arriving?

90 days from entry for temporary visa holders (30 days from the residence authorization's publication for some categories). You receive the CRNM foreigner ID card after registering.

How do I get a CPF as a foreigner?

Residents apply in person at Banco do Brasil, Caixa Economica Federal or Correios for a small fee; those abroad apply at a Brazilian consulate or through the Receita Federal's online e-CAC service.

Is public healthcare (SUS) free for foreign residents?

Yes. SUS is free and universal for everyone present in Brazil, resident or not, though many expats add private insurance to shorten specialist waiting times.

When do I become a Brazilian tax resident?

After 183 days of presence within a rolling 12-month period, or immediately on arrival if you hold a permanent, work, retirement, investor or digital nomad residence visa. Residents are taxed on worldwide income.

Can foreigners buy property in Brazil?

Yes, urban property can be bought freely with a CPF. Rural land purchases are capped and require prior authorization, and land within 150km of a land border needs National Defence Council clearance.

What is the minimum pension for the retirement visa?

At least USD 2,000/month, transferable to a Brazilian account, plus USD 1,000 per additional dependant, under the VITEM XIV / VIPER route.

What is the minimum investment for Brazil's investor visa?

BRL 500,000 into a Brazilian company, or BRL 1,000,000 in urban real estate (reduced by up to 30% in North and Northeast regions) under RN 36.
