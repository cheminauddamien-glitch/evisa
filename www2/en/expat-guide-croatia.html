Expat Guide to Croatia 2026: Nomads, Retirees & the Adriatic Coast

Croatia pairs full Schengen and eurozone membership with one of Europe's most generous digital nomad rules: up to 18 months of tax-free residence for remote workers. Add an Adriatic coastline running from Zadar to Split and Dubrovnik, EU-standard HZZO healthcare, and affordable coastal living outside the July-August peak, and Croatia has become a serious base for nomads, retirees and property buyers, not just a summer destination.

Who Croatia Suits Best

Remote workers who want an EU, Schengen and eurozone base with a purpose-built, tax-free digital nomad permit rather than tourist-visa hopping. Retirees drawn to Adriatic coastal towns and Zagreb's EU-standard healthcare who can show a stable pension or investment income. Property buyers from reciprocity countries looking for a second home outside the summer crowds. It is a weaker fit for anyone chasing fast local employment: non-EU work permits run on an annual quota, and nomad income must come strictly from abroad.

Step-by-Step

  1. Identify the route that matches your situation (digital nomad, other purposes, Blue Card, quota work permit, family reunification).
  2. Apply online via the MUP e-Građani portal where available, or at the Croatian embassy/consulate with jurisdiction over your residence.
  3. Submit income or savings evidence, health insurance, accommodation proof, and, for nomads, proof of remote work for a non-Croatian employer plus a clean criminal record certificate.
  4. Pay the fee: from EUR 46.45 at a police station up to EUR 55.74 (stay) plus EUR 93.00 (visa D) at a consulate.
  5. Enter Croatia and, if required, report your residence address within 3 days.
  6. Collect your biometric residence permit card from the police administration.
  7. Get your OIB from the Tax Administration and register with HZZO or arrange private insurance.
  8. Renew before expiry, or observe the required waiting period before reapplying.

Pro Tip: EU/EEA/Swiss citizens need none of the above: register at the local police administration only if staying past 90 days.

Digital Nomads & Remote Work

Croatia's digital nomad temporary stay targets third-country nationals employed by, or self-employed for, a company not registered in Croatia and not serving Croatian clients. The 2026 income threshold is EUR 3,622.50/month, set at 2.5 times Croatia's average net salary the previous year per the Bureau of Statistics, rising 10% per accompanying family member; applicants can instead show savings of EUR 43,470 for 12 months or EUR 65,205 for 18 months. The stay runs up to 18 months, with one further extension of up to 6 months if applied for within 60 days of expiry, after which a 6-month wait, generally outside Croatia, applies before reapplying.

Foreign-sourced income under this status is exempt from Croatian income tax for the life of the permit under Article 9(1)(26) of the Income Tax Act, and holders skip Croatian pension/health contributions on it, so private international health insurance is essential.

Note: The permit does not authorise work for a Croatian employer or local clients; qualifying income must originate outside Croatia.

Working Locally

A payroll job with a Croatian employer generally needs either the EU Blue Card for highly qualified roles above an annual salary threshold, or a standard work permit drawn from the government's annual quota by sector. Self-employment outside those routes typically means registering a Croatian business (obrt or d.o.o.). Freelancing for Croatian clients while on a digital nomad or other-purposes permit is not permitted; those routes are strictly for foreign-sourced income or no local work. EU/EEA/Swiss citizens can work in Croatia without any permit.

Retirement

Croatia has no dedicated retirement visa. Retirees living on a pension, investments or rental income apply instead for temporary stay for other purposes, which requires proof of sufficient means to live without working plus valid health insurance. The permit is granted for up to one year at a time; renewing before it expires keeps your stay continuous, while letting it lapse means waiting 6 months before a fresh application. After 5 years of continuous, legally approved temporary stay, retirees can apply for permanent residence, which removes the need for annual renewal.

Pro Tip: EU/EEA/Swiss pensioners avoid this route entirely: register your address with the local police once you pass 90 days and you are set for the long term.

Family & Dependants

Spouses, registered partners and dependent children can join a permit holder through family reunification, or be included directly in a digital nomad application, which raises the required income by 10% per accompanying family member. Each family member needs their own residence card and, once eligible, HZZO enrolment or private insurance. School-age children can attend the free public system alongside Croatian pupils or an international school; confirm current enrolment steps with your local school administration.

Healthcare

Public Healthcare (HZZO)

The Croatian Health Insurance Fund gives insured residents a family doctor and access to public hospitals and clinics; standards are generally good in Zagreb and larger coastal cities, thinner in smaller islands and rural areas, especially in summer.

Private Healthcare

Private clinics in Zagreb, Split and Zadar offer shorter waits and English-speaking staff, commonly used by expats without automatic HZZO cover, such as digital nomads and other-purposes residents.

Registration is with a local HZZO regional office once you hold an eligible residence status; changes must be reported within 8 days.

Health Insurance

HZZO compulsory insurance is mandatory for permanent and long-term residents, most work-based temporary stays, and EU/EEA/Swiss citizens with temporary stay who are not insured elsewhere. Digital nomads and other-purposes residents fall outside this compulsory cover, since they do not pay Croatian contributions, so a private policy meeting the visa's minimum coverage is required both to apply and to stay covered. HZZO also applies patient cost-sharing on services not fully covered, from a minimum of EUR 1.32 up to a cap of EUR 530.88 per invoice; check the official portal for the current voluntary-contribution base if you plan to join HZZO as a non-active self-payer.

Pro Tip: Compare a private international policy against voluntary HZZO enrolment before deciding; for retirees the private route is often faster to arrange from abroad.

Banking

Opening a Croatian account needs an OIB and proof of address; major retail banks (Zagrebačka banka, Privredna banka Zagreb, Erste, OTP, Raiffeisen) all serve expats, with a branch visit and residence permit generally required for a full current account. Challenger apps such as Revolut and N26 are widely used for the first weeks before local paperwork clears, and euro membership means no currency conversion on everyday spending.

Tax Residency

You become a Croatian tax resident with a registered residence or habitual abode in Croatia, generally evidenced by spending more than 183 days in the country in a tax year, per the Income Tax Act administered by the Tax Administration (Porezna uprava). Residents owe tax on worldwide income, with a carve-out for digital nomad permit holders, whose qualifying foreign-sourced income stays exempt regardless of days spent (see Digital Nomads).

Each municipality adds its own surtax-equivalent rate of 0-15 percentage points on top of the national rate, so the combined rate varies by address (Zagreb, Split and Rijeka commonly cite combined bands around 17-28%); a monthly personal allowance of EUR 560 reduces taxable income for residents.

Note: Tax residency and exemptions are case-specific; confirm your situation with the Tax Administration or a licensed advisor before relocating.

Housing: Renting & Buying

Renting is straightforward with an OIB and proof of income, though coastal listings swing sharply between the off-season and the June-September peak. EU, EEA and Swiss citizens can buy property under the same conditions as Croatian nationals. Non-EU citizens need reciprocity between Croatia and their home country and prior consent from the Ministry of Justice and Public Administration for each purchase; approval covers houses, apartments and building land but not agricultural or non-urbanised land, and buying alone does not grant a residence permit.

Pro Tip: Check the current reciprocity list with the Ministry of Justice and Public Administration before making an offer; approval timelines for non-EU buyers can run several months.

Best Cities & Areas for Expats

Zagreb
Capital, best job market and business infrastructure, largest expat and international-school community, year-round living.
Split
Adriatic hub, ferries to the islands, wide European flight links, a growing nomad and coworking scene around Diocletian's Palace.
Zadar
Smaller and cheaper than Split or Dubrovnik, walkable old town, popular with remote workers wanting the coast without peak-season crowds.
Dubrovnik
Most expensive city, especially June-September, but Old Town heritage and flight links suit retirees who winter there.

Close to Italy and Slovenia by road, established retiree community, milder low-season climate than further south.

Transport & Driving Licence

Trains and long-distance buses link Zagreb to the interior; the coast relies more on buses and Jadrolinija ferries connecting Split, Zadar, Dubrovnik and the islands. EU/EEA/Swiss driving licences remain valid throughout your stay; non-EU licence holders can drive on their foreign licence for up to a year after establishing residence, after which the licence generally must be exchanged for a Croatian one at a police administration, usually without a new test where a bilateral agreement applies. Confirm the current exchange procedure for your nationality with the official portal.

Schools & Education

Public schools are free and compulsory from age 6 to 15, open to resident foreign children through the local school administration alongside Croatian pupils, generally taught in Croatian. International schools with British, American or IB curricula operate in Zagreb and along the coast for families wanting continuity with a home-country system, at fees typically running several thousand to over EUR 15,000/year.

Phone & Internet

Prepaid SIM cards from Hrvatski Telekom, A1 or Telemach require passport ID at purchase and are inexpensive by EU standards; eSIMs are widely supported in cities. Fibre and cable broadband are common in Zagreb and larger coastal towns, generally sufficient for video calls and remote work, though smaller islands can still see slower or seasonal connections.

Croatia is generally very safe, with low violent-crime rates; the main practical risks are minor theft in dense tourist areas in summer and busy coastal roads during the peak season, so keep travel insurance current and drive cautiously on narrow Adriatic routes.

Moving Checklist

  • Confirm the right route (digital nomad, other purposes, Blue Card, quota work permit, family reunification) and gather documents.
  • Apply via the MUP e-Građani portal or your nearest Croatian consulate.
  • Book flexible short-term housing for your first weeks, especially off the peak coastal season.
  • Report your residence address within 3 days of arrival if required for your route.
  • Get your OIB from the Tax Administration.
  • Collect your biometric residence permit card from the police administration.
  • Register with HZZO if eligible, or arrange private health insurance.
  • Open a bank account and, if you plan to drive long-term, arrange your licence exchange.

Pros & Cons

Pros

  • Tax-free digital nomad permit up to 18 months, no currency risk inside the eurozone
  • Full Schengen membership for easy regional travel
  • EU-standard HZZO healthcare once eligible, strong private-clinic alternative
  • Affordable Adriatic coastal living outside July-August

Cons

  • No dedicated retirement visa; retirees rely on the general "other purposes" route
  • Non-EU property purchases need reciprocity plus Ministry of Justice approval
  • Local employment routes are quota-limited and paperwork-heavy
  • Coastal prices and rents spike sharply in the summer season

FAQ

How much do I need to earn for Croatia's digital nomad temporary stay?

The 2026 threshold is at least EUR 3,622.50/month, equal to 2.5 times the average net salary paid in Croatia the previous year per state statistics, plus 10% for each accompanying family member. You may show savings instead: EUR 43,470 for a 12-month stay or EUR 65,205 for 18 months.

How long can a digital nomad stay in Croatia, and can I renew?

Up to 18 months on the first grant, plus one extension of up to 6 months applied for within 60 days of expiry. After the stay ends you must wait 6 months, generally outside Croatia, before submitting a new digital nomad application.

Do digital nomads pay Croatian income tax on their foreign salary?

No. Under Article 9(1)(26) of the Income Tax Act, income earned through employment or self-employment for an employer not registered in Croatia, received on the basis of digital nomad status, is exempt from Croatian income tax for the life of the permit.

When did Croatia join Schengen and adopt the euro?

Both on 1 January 2023. Land and sea border checks with other Schengen states were lifted that day; air border checks followed on 26 March 2023. The kuna was replaced by the euro at a fixed rate of 7.53450 kuna to 1 euro.

Is healthcare free for expats in Croatia?

HZZO compulsory insurance is mandatory and largely free at the point of use for permanent and long-term residents and most work-based temporary stays. Digital nomads and other non-working temporary residents are not covered automatically and need private health insurance instead.

Can non-EU citizens buy property in Croatia?

Yes, but only with reciprocity between Croatia and the buyer's home country and prior consent from the Ministry of Justice and Public Administration for each purchase. EU, EEA and Swiss citizens can buy under the same conditions as Croatian nationals, with no such approval needed.

Is there a dedicated retirement visa for Croatia?

No. Retirees living on a pension or other passive income normally apply for temporary stay for other purposes, granted for up to one year at a time and renewed annually, provided they show sufficient means and valid health insurance.

When can I apply for permanent residence in Croatia?

After 5 years of continuous, legally approved temporary stay, without being absent more than 10 months total or 6 months at once; 4 years if married to a Croatian citizen. Some short-term permit categories may not count, so confirm your route with mup.gov.hr.
