Live & Work in Germany 2026: Complete Expat Guide

Germany runs one of Europe's clearest skilled-migration systems: the EU Blue Card for job-holders, the points-based Opportunity Card for job-seekers, and a dedicated freelance permit for the self-employed. Add strong labour protections, statutory health cover and a straightforward Anmeldung process, and Germany is a practical, if paperwork-heavy, base for professionals moving to the EU.

Who Germany Is Best For

Germany suits skilled employees with a job offer or a shortage-occupation profile (IT, engineering, healthcare, STEM), graduates of German universities, and freelancers who can show real client demand. It works less well for retirees, since there is no dedicated retirement visa, and for casual remote workers, since Germany has no digital-nomad visa either — non-EU nomads generally need an employer, a freelance permit or the Opportunity Card.

Visa & Residency Routes

Step-by-Step Process (Blue Card or Skilled Worker route)

  1. Secure a job offer or, for the Opportunity Card, gather your points evidence
  2. Apply for a national (D) visa at the German embassy or consulate, or via the digital visa portal where available
  3. Enter Germany and complete the Anmeldung (address registration) within 14 days
  4. Book an appointment at the local Auslaenderbehoerde (foreigners' office) to convert the visa into a residence permit
  5. Register for statutory or private health insurance and open a bank account
  6. Collect your electronic residence card (eAT) and, for the Blue Card, apply for settlement after 21-27 months
Pro Tip: The Blue Card general threshold needs no Bundesagentur für Arbeit approval; the lower shortage-occupation threshold does, since the agency checks your salary and hours match German norms.

Digital Nomads & Remote Work

Germany has no digital-nomad visa. Working remotely for a foreign employer while on a Schengen short stay is not authorised for extended periods, and tax residency rules bite after 183 days. Realistic options for non-EU remote workers are the Opportunity Card (job-search, part-time work only), the freelance permit if you can show German or EU clients, or a standard work visa if your employer has a German entity. EU/EEA citizens can simply relocate and work remotely without a permit.

Working Locally

Employees are protected by strong labour law: written contracts, statutory paid leave (typically 20-30 days), continued pay during sickness, and notice periods that lengthen with tenure. The statutory minimum wage from 1 January 2026 is €13.90/hour gross, rising to €14.60 in 2027. Non-EU workers on a Blue Card, skilled-worker or Chancenkarte permit are tied to specific work conditions on the permit; check with your Auslaenderbehoerde before changing employer or role.

Retirement

Germany has no dedicated retirement visa. EU/EEA/Swiss retirees can settle freely. Non-EU retirees generally cannot obtain long-term residence purely on pension income — the closest routes are family reunification with a resident relative, a self-employment permit (which still requires the age-45+ retirement-provision proof above), or a national visa tied to another legal basis such as German ancestry. Foreign pensions paid to German residents are usually taxable in Germany; check any double-taxation treaty with your home country.

Family & Dependants

Spouses and minor children of a valid permit holder can apply for family reunification (Familiennachzug). Spouses generally need basic A1 German before the visa is issued, with exceptions for EU citizens, nationals of the US, Canada, Japan, South Korea, Australia, Israel and New Zealand, and for university graduates or Blue Card/skilled-worker family members in some cases. Children under 16 usually join without a language test. Dependants get access to public schooling and statutory health insurance once registered.

Healthcare

Statutory system (GKV)

Covers roughly 90% of residents: employees, students and most self-employed under the income threshold are compulsorily insured. Care is high quality with short waits for urgent issues; family doctors (Hausarzt) act as gatekeepers to specialists.

Private system (PKV)

Open to freelancers, high earners above the compulsory-insurance threshold and civil servants. Faster specialist access and single rooms, but premiums rise with age and pre-existing conditions are underwritten at entry.

Health Insurance

Health insurance is compulsory for every resident from day one. Employees are automatically enrolled in the GKV through payroll unless they qualify to opt out. In 2026 the compulsory-insurance threshold (Jahresarbeitsentgeltgrenze) is €77,400/year (€6,450/month): employees above it may switch to private cover, though most stay in GKV for its family-inclusive, no-underwriting model. The combined GKV contribution is 14.6% general rate plus an average 2.9% supplementary rate, split between employer and employee.

Freelancers and the self-employed

Freelancers can choose GKV or PKV regardless of income; GKV premiums for the self-employed are based on declared profit, while PKV premiums depend on age and health. A short-term international policy is often used to bridge the gap before a German policy starts.

Pro Tip: Compare at least three GKV funds (Krankenkassen) before enrolling: the base rate is fixed nationally, but the supplementary contribution varies by fund and can differ by more than 2 percentage points.

Banking

A German IBAN (or any SEPA account) is enough for rent, salary and utilities. Most residence-permit applications and rental contracts accept an EU-wide SEPA account, so an online bank (N26, DKB, Deutsche Bank, Sparkasse, Commerzbank) works from day one for most residents. You will typically need your passport, Anmeldung certificate (Meldebescheinigung) and, for some traditional banks, your tax ID.

Opening steps

  1. Complete the Anmeldung first to get the Meldebescheinigung
  2. Choose an online bank for speed or a branch bank (Sparkasse) for in-person service and cash deposits
  3. Verify identity via video-ident, PostIdent at a post office, or in-branch
  4. Receive your IBAN immediately online, or a card by post within days

Residents who spend more than 183 days/year in Germany, or keep their main home there, are taxed on worldwide income. Everyone gets a lifelong tax ID (Steuer-ID) automatically on first registration, used for payroll, banking and filing.

Key points

  • Tax classes (Steuerklasse I-VI) set monthly payroll withholding by marital/employment status, not your final annual bill
  • Church tax (Kirchensteuer) applies only to registered members of a recognised church: 8% of income tax in Bavaria/Baden-Wuerttemberg, 9% elsewhere
  • Social security (pension, health, unemployment, long-term care) is withheld alongside income tax for employees
  • Freelancers file an annual return (Einkommensteuererklaerung) and usually register for VAT (Umsatzsteuer) above the small-business threshold
Germany taxes worldwide income for residents; check any double-taxation treaty with your home country before you relocate, and confirm your VAT obligations with the Finanzamt if you invoice as a freelancer.

Housing: Renting & Buying

Most expats rent. Demand in major cities is intense, so a strong application — Schufa credit report, recent payslips or an employment contract, and a Mieterselbstauskunft (tenant self-disclosure form) — matters more than nationality. The deposit (Kaution) is capped by law at 3 months' net (cold) rent and can be paid in up to 3 monthly instalments.

Renting checklist

  • Schufa report (credit history) — get one early, it takes days to arrive
  • Proof of income: last 3 payslips or an employment contract
  • Mietschuldenfreiheitsbescheinigung (proof of no rent arrears) if you rented before in Germany
  • Kaltmiete (cold rent) vs. Warmmiete (rent with utilities/heating) — always compare like for like

Buying property

No restrictions on foreign buyers. Budget notary and land-registry fees (~1.5-2%), real-estate transfer tax (Grunderwerbsteuer, 3.5-6.5% depending on the Land) and agent commission where applicable, on top of the purchase price.

Best Cities for Expats

Berlin
The most international and (relatively) affordable of the three; large English-speaking, freelance and startup community. New-listing rents average roughly €16-22/sqm (indicative, editorial estimate, September 2026).
Munich
Highest salaries and the strongest job market for engineering, tech and finance, but Germany's most expensive rents, averaging roughly €25-26/sqm (indicative, editorial estimate, September 2026).
Hamburg
Port city with strong media, logistics and trade sectors; rents sit between Berlin and Munich, averaging roughly €23-24/sqm (indicative, editorial estimate, September 2026).

Transport & Driving Licence

Public transport is extensive and reliable; the nationwide Deutschlandticket (€49/month) covers regional trains, buses, trams and the U-Bahn/S-Bahn in every city. Driving licences from EU/EEA countries remain valid. Licences from other countries are usually valid for 6 months after registering residence, after which a conversion (Umschreibung) is required — some countries have a reciprocal agreement allowing a simple exchange, others require theory and practical tests.

Schools & Education

Public schools are free and compulsory from age 6, taught in German, with quality varying by Land (state) rather than city. International and bilingual private schools exist in all three major cities (roughly €800-1,500+/month) for families wanting English-language continuity. Public universities charge little to no tuition for most degree programmes, including for many non-EU students, aside from a modest semester contribution.

Phone & Internet

Prepaid SIMs (Telekom, Vodafone, O2, or budget brands like congstar and Aldi Talk) are available at kiosks and supermarkets with just a passport; registration is required by law. Postpaid contracts usually need a German bank account and Anmeldung. Home fibre/DSL plans run roughly €30-50/month; availability and speed vary by building and city.

Safety & Emergency Numbers

Germany is a low-violent-crime country by international standards; petty theft (pickpocketing, bike theft) is the main risk in big cities. The EU-wide emergency number 112 reaches police, fire and ambulance free from any phone; the non-emergency police line is 110.

Moving Checklist

  1. Apply for your visa/permit and gather qualification, financial and insurance proofs
  2. Book temporary housing for the first weeks; permanent leases are hard to secure remotely
  3. Complete the Anmeldung within 14 days of moving in
  4. Open a bank account and get your tax ID and health insurance sorted
  5. Book your Auslaenderbehoerde appointment for the residence permit/card
  6. Start German lessons early — A1/B1 unlocks faster settlement and better jobs

FAQ

What is the minimum salary for the EU Blue Card in Germany in 2026?

EUR 50,700/year gross for most occupations, or EUR 45,934.20/year for shortage occupations, IT specialists without a degree, and recent graduates.

What is the Opportunity Card (Chancenkarte) and who needs it?

A points-based, one-year residence permit for skilled non-EU professionals to search for a job in Germany without a prior offer. It requires at least 6 points and proof of EUR 1,091/month in a blocked account.

Can I get a freelance visa for Germany as a non-EU citizen?

Yes, under Sec. 21 AufenthG, if you can show a viable business plan and demand for your services (often via letters of intent from German clients). Applicants over 45 must also prove adequate retirement provision.

How soon do I need to register my address (Anmeldung) after moving to Germany?

Within 14 days of moving into your residence. The resulting Meldebescheinigung is required to open a bank account, get a tax ID and register for health insurance.

Do I need private or statutory health insurance in Germany?

Most residents use statutory insurance (GKV). Employees earning above EUR 77,400/year (2026) may opt for private insurance (PKV); freelancers can choose either regardless of income.

Does Germany have a digital nomad visa?

No. Non-EU remote workers generally need an employer-sponsored visa, a freelance permit with German/EU clients, or the Opportunity Card while job-hunting.

Is there a retirement visa for Germany?

No dedicated retirement visa exists. EU/EEA/Swiss retirees can settle freely; non-EU retirees generally need another basis, such as family reunification.

How much deposit (Kaution) can a landlord ask for in Germany?

By law, at most 3 months' net (cold) rent, payable in up to 3 equal monthly instalments under Sec. 551 BGB.
