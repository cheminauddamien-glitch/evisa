Complete Expat Guide to the Philippines 2026 : Retire, Work & Live in the Islands

The Philippines is the only Asian country where English is an official working language. Add a 30-day visa-free entry that can legally stretch to 36 months, an SRRV deposit from USD 15,000, and island living in Cebu or Dumaguete under USD 1,500 a month. Below are the current 2026 rules, checked against Philippine government sources.

Who the Philippines is best for

Unusually generous on entry and language, unusually strict on property.

  • Retirees 40+: the SRRV Classic asks USD 15,000-50,000 and grants indefinite residence.
  • Native English speakers: hospitals, banks and immigration counters all work in English.
  • Remote workers wanting a long runway: visa-exempt nationals can extend a stay to 36 months.
  • Spouses of Filipino citizens: the 13(a) visa needs no deposit, permanent residence in a year.
  • Island lifestyles: Cebu, Bohol, Dumaguete and Siargao have daily flights and low rents.

Visa & residency routes

The Bureau of Immigration (BI) handles status inside the country, the DFA issues visas abroad, and the PRA runs retirement residence. Every arrival must complete free eTravel registration first.

From arrival to resident card

  1. Register free on eTravel within 72 hours of your flight.
  2. Arrive on the 30-day visa-free entry, or a 9(a) visa if required.
  3. Pick your target status before day 25; convert in-country rather than overstaying.
  4. SRRV: register with the PRA and remit the deposit. 9(g): the employer files the AEP, then petitions the BI.
  5. Collect the ACR I-Card, then a BIR number and PhilHealth.
Pro Tip: A visa-exempt national can live in Cebu three years on tourist status with no income proof, but it gives no tax residence, no PhilHealth and no path to permanent residence.

Digital nomads & remote work

Executive Order 86 (2025) gave remote work a legal basis: the DFA may issue a one-year, once-renewable visa to foreigners 18+ working remotely for clients outside the Philippines, with proof of income, insurance and a clean record.

With no live channel yet, most nomads still use the 9(a) extension ladder. Fibre reaches Cebu City, Dumaguete and Siargao's General Luna, Starlink covers smaller islands, and coworking clusters sit in BGC and Cebu IT Park.

Note: Do not budget against an income figure from a relocation blog: EO 86 published none. Check dfa.gov.ph, and keep the 9(a) ladder as a fallback.

Working locally

You cannot take a Philippine job on tourist status: DOLE issues the AEP only once it finds no qualified Filipino for the role.

ItemWhat applies
Alien Employment PermitFiled with DOLE. PHP 10,000 for one year, plus PHP 5,000 per extra year.
9(g) visaFiled with the BI by the employer, for one, two or three years.
Reserved professionsLaw, medicine, engineering, nursing, accountancy reserved to Filipinos except under reciprocity.
Business ownershipRetail is restricted under the Negative List; consultancy and BPO are open.
Pro Tip: SRRV holders may work, but still need a DOLE permit for a local job. The SRRV removes the immigration barrier, not the labour-market one.

Retirement: the SRRV in detail

The SRRV is why most foreigners settle here: place a fixed sum in an accredited bank and get indefinite residence, no annual renewal. A September 2025 restructure left only Classic and Courtesy, dropped the minimum age to 40, and retired the old Smile/Human Touch tiers.

SRRV optionAge and depositConditions
Classic, pensioner50+: USD 15,000. 40-49: USD 25,000Verified pension of USD 800/month single, USD 1,000 with dependants.
Classic, non-pensioner50+: USD 30,000. 40-49: USD 50,000No pension needed; covers principal plus two dependants.
Courtesy50+: USD 1,500. 40-49: USD 3,000-6,000Retired diplomats, international-organisation officers, ex-military, former Filipinos.

With PRA approval the deposit can convert into a qualifying investment such as a long condominium lease, and SRRV retirees pay a flat annual PhilHealth premium.

Pro Tip: Never remit the deposit before the PRA accepts your file: paying an agent or an unaccredited account is how applicants lose money.

Family & dependants

Marriage to a Filipino citizen is the cheapest residence path open to any foreigner.

  • 13(a): one probationary year, then permanent residence, no deposit or income threshold.
  • SRRV dependants: two included; each further one adds USD 15,000 to the deposit.
  • Airport trap: minors leaving without a parent need DSWD clearance.

Healthcare

Healthcare runs at two speeds: good private hospitals in Metro Manila and Cebu, basic provincial public facilities. On the islands, plan for evacuation to Cebu or Manila.

PhilHealth and public care

PhilHealth is the national insurance scheme, mandatory for Filipinos and open to foreign residents. It pays fixed case rates rather than full costs, reducing a bill rather than clearing it.

Private hospitals

St. Luke's and Makati Medical Center in Metro Manila, plus Chong Hua and Cebu Doctors' in Cebu, are what expats use. Standards are high; payment is expected up front.

Health insurance

PhilHealth alone is not enough: a floor under household costs, not comprehensive cover. Most residents run PhilHealth plus a local HMO or an international policy.

PhilHealth for foreign residents
Resident-visa holders may enrol. 2026 guidance: PHP 15,000/year for PRA/SRRV retirees, PHP 17,000 for others; confirm at philhealth.gov.ph.
Maxicare and PhilCare
The two largest HMOs, accepted at St. Luke's and Makati Med. Default for resident expats under 60.
International policies
Cigna Global, Allianz Care and April International, for cover abroad and island medical evacuation.
Pro Tip: Local HMOs often refuse new members above 60-65 and exclude pre-existing conditions for a year. Moving at retirement age, buy an international policy while you still qualify abroad.

Banking

Opening a peso account is paperwork-heavy; the decisive document is the ACR I-Card, and most branches refuse a plain tourist stamp.

What branches ask for

  • Passport with your current visa or admission stamp
  • ACR I-Card, effectively mandatory
  • Proof of Philippine address and a BIR tax number if receiving local income
  • Two ID photos and an initial deposit of PHP 5,000-10,000

Practical sequence

  1. Secure long-stay status and the ACR I-Card first; do not start with the bank.
  2. Choose a large branch: BDO, BPI, Metrobank and Security Bank all handle foreign clients.
  3. Add GCash or Maya for rent, utilities, ferries and BI e-services fees.
Pro Tip: Do not confuse the SRRV deposit with a spending account: it sits locked as a visa condition, so open a current account separately.

Tax residency

The Philippines quietly outperforms rival retirement destinations here: a resident alien is taxed only on Philippine-source income, not worldwide income, so a foreign pension falls outside the local base.

Points that matter

  • Stay over 180 days a year and you are taxed at graduated rates on Philippine-source income, as engaged in trade or business.
  • A non-resident alien not engaged in trade or business pays a flat 25% on gross Philippine-source income, no exempt band.
Note: None of this is tax advice. Your position depends on your citizenship and any double-taxation treaty as much as on Philippine law: take advice in both countries.

Housing: renting & buying

The Constitution reserves land to Filipino citizens and companies at least 60% Filipino-owned, no exception for marriage; a condominium unit is what a foreigner can own outright.

  • Condominium units: allowed under RA 4726, within a 40% foreign-ownership cap per project.
  • Land: closed to foreigners, even a house-and-lot in a foreigner's own name.
  • Long lease: RA 12252 (2025) replaced the old 50+25-year rule with a single lease up to 99 years for BOI-approved foreign investors.
  • Through a spouse: land is titled to the Filipino spouse only; plan succession with a lawyer.

Transport & driving licence

Distances are measured in flights and ferries, not road hours: Cebu Pacific, Philippine Airlines and AirAsia connect the islands cheaply; Metro Manila traffic is among the world's worst.

  • Foreign licence: valid in English briefly, then the LTO requires conversion; confirm the current window.
  • Motorcycles: the practical island vehicle, and the leading cause of expat medical evacuation.
  • Typhoon season: roughly June to November; ferries and regional flights cancel at short notice.
Pro Tip: Fly, do not drive, between regions. Manila to Cebu takes 80 minutes and often costs less than fuel plus ferry, skipping the roads where serious accidents happen.

Phone & internet

Connectivity is improving fast but stays uneven between a fibre-served block and a barangay two islands away.

  • Networks: Globe, Smart and DITO, good in cities, patchy on small islands.
  • SIM registration: mandatory under the SIM Registration Act, done with your passport.
  • Fibre: PLDT Home, Globe At Home and Converge, typically PHP 1,700-2,900/month.
  • Starlink: available and the standard backup outside the fibre footprint.

Moving checklist

Order matters. Doing these out of sequence turns a two-week settling-in into a three-month one.

  1. Pick your route and confirm its 2026 conditions on the agency's own site.
  2. Get a police clearance and, for the SRRV, apostilled certificates before leaving.
  3. Buy insurance covering island medical evacuation, and register on eTravel.
  4. Lodge the SRRV, 9(g) or 13(a) application in country; extend 9(a) until granted.
  5. Collect the ACR I-Card, then open the bank account, then sign the lease.
  6. Diarise the BI Annual Report for January-March and the annual PRA fee.

Pros & cons

What works

  • English everywhere, in hospitals, banks, leases and government offices
  • The longest legal tourist-status runway in ASEAN, up to 36 months
  • SRRV deposits from USD 15,000 with indefinite residence
  • Resident aliens taxed only on Philippine-source income

What to weigh

  • No land ownership for foreigners, not even through marriage
  • Thin public healthcare outside the big cities, costly island evacuation
  • Electricity among the most expensive in the region
  • Typhoons, earthquakes and volcanoes as routine planning factors

Frequently asked questions

How long can I stay in the Philippines without a visa?

Nationals of about 150 countries get 30 days on arrival under EO 408. A 29-day waiver takes that to 59 days, and further extensions can reach 36 months for visa-exempt nationals, 24 for visa-required ones. Chinese nationals get a separate, non-extendable 14-day entry since 16 January 2026, via Manila or Cebu only.

What is the minimum deposit for the SRRV?

SRRV Classic: a pensioner 50+ deposits USD 15,000 with a pension of at least USD 800/month; a non-pensioner deposits USD 30,000. The 40-49 band pays USD 25,000 or USD 50,000, plus a USD 1,500 processing fee.

Does the Philippines have a digital nomad visa in 2026?

Yes in law, not yet in practice. Executive Order 86 (2025) authorises a one-year, once-renewable visa, but the DFA has no live application channel as of September 2026. Check dfa.gov.ph.

Can foreigners buy property in the Philippines?

Condominium units yes, land no. The Constitution reserves land to Filipino citizens and companies at least 60% Filipino-owned. In a condo project, foreign ownership must stay within 40% of total floor area. Land can now be leased for up to 99 years under RA 12252 (2025).

Will I pay Philippine tax on my foreign pension?

Generally no. A resident alien is taxed only on Philippine-source income; worldwide taxation applies to resident citizens, not foreign residents. Your home country may still tax the pension at source.

Can a foreign resident join PhilHealth?

Yes. Resident-visa holders can enrol; 2026 guidance sets a flat annual premium of PHP 15,000 for PRA/SRRV retirees and PHP 17,000 for others. It covers case-rate hospital costs, not outpatient care, so most add an HMO.
